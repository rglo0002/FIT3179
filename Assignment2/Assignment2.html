Satellites of Earth

Exploring the Operational Satellites in Earth Orbit (as of May 2023)

Satellites Over Time

The first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, was launched by the Soviet Union in 1957, marking the beginning of the space age. Since then, a significant amount of satellites have been sent to space, with 7,560 operational satellites currently in orbit. Many more than that number have been launched, but are no longer operational.

Satellites are launched for a variety of purposes, including communication, Earth observation, and scientific research. There are 4 major categories of earth orbits, ranked in order of how far away they are: Low Earth Orbit (LEO), Medium Earth Orbit (MEO), Geostationary Orbit (GEO), and Elliptical.

Satellites usually have a lifespan of 5 to 15 years, depending on the type of satellite and its mission. However, the oldest satellite still in operation is the amateur AMSAT-Oscar A07, which launched in 1974. It was suspected that it died in 1981 but miraculously came back to life in 2002. It is still operational today [1].

A significant shift in satellite mass began in 2020, with SpaceX commencing their 'Starlink' program. This program aims to provide global internet coverage through a constellation of satellites in Low Earth Orbit (LEO).

Satellite Distribution by Country

America (USA) is the country with the most satellites in orbit, with 4,848 operational satellites. This is followed by the United Kingdom and then China. The majority of these satellites are in Low Earth Orbit (LEO), and in fact 3,995 (82.4%) of them are Starlink satellites.

SpaceX has been able to rapidly increase the number of satellites launched over the last few years due to their reusable rocket technology.

Countries have partnered to launch a number of satellites over the years, with the European Space Agency (ESA) being the most prominent. The ESA has 48 active satellites in orbit, with the majority of them being Earth Observation satellites.

Earth Observation satellites are used to monitor the Earth's surface, atmosphere, and oceans, providing valuable data for climate change research and disaster management.

Investors in Satellite Technology & Their Uses

The 4 major categories of those who invest in satellite technology are the government, commercial, civil, and military sectors.

Satellites in Earth orbit serve a wide range of applications depending on their altitude, ranging from Low Earth Orbit (LEO) to Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO). In LEO, satellites typically orbit between 160 to 2,000 kilometers above the Earth’s surface and are used for Earth observation, remote sensing, scientific research, and communication constellations like Starlink. Their proximity allows high-resolution imaging and low-latency communications.

In Medium Earth Orbit (MEO), satellites at altitudes of 2,000 to 35,786 kilometers are often used for global navigation systems, such as GPS, providing positioning and timing services. In GEO, satellites orbit at about 35,786 kilometers and appear stationary relative to Earth, making them ideal for telecommunications, weather monitoring, and broadcast services, as they can cover large areas continuously. These orbits offer unique advantages depending on the mission, such as persistent coverage in GEO or detailed imaging in LEO.

Satellites by Launch Vehicle

Falcon 9 is a groundbreaking rocket known for its reusability, allowing its first stage to land and be reused for multiple missions. This innovation has drastically reduced launch costs, making it a leading option for satellite deployment. Meanwhile, India's PSLV (Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle) is prized for its versatility, frequently launching multiple satellites into different orbits in one mission, particularly to polar orbits.

China’s Long March family of rockets, including the Long March 2C and 3B, plays a major role in deploying satellites across various orbits, from LEO to geostationary orbit (GEO). Finally, the Electron rocket from Rocket Lab focuses on delivering lightweight payloads to LEO, providing frequent and flexible launches for smaller satellites, making it a top choice for miniaturized payloads.
